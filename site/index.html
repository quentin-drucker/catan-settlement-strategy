About the Project

This project is a study of initial settlement placement in the game Settlers of Catan, where our goal is to determine the 'fairness' of each seat in the placement draft and to what extent different draft types affect this metric.

Background

In Settlers of Catan the board is a set of hexagonal tiles, each marked with a number from 2 to 12 but excluding 7. Every turn two dice are rolled, and every settlement next to a tile with that number collects resources. Numbers near 7 come up most often: a 6 or an 8 can each be rolled 5 ways out of 36, a 2 or a 12 only one way. The printed tokens show this with dots under the number representing their probabilities.

Settlements sit on the corners where tiles meet, and two settlements may never be on neighbouring corners, occupy the same corner, or be moved/destroyed; so the opening placement matters a lot for each player's game trajectory. The order of the opening placement is decided with a single dice roll from each player, usually played where the highest roll goes first.

That creates a problem where the player who places first gets the best spot on the board. The official rules already respond to this with something called a “snake draft”, which is effective because each player must place two settlements. A snake draft dictates the initial highest first order happens first, and then its inverse happens. So, the player who places first also places last, and the player who rolled the lowest starting number places both of their settlements at the same time.

In game-theory terms, the opening is a finite sequential game with perfect information. Players move one at a time with everyone seeing every earlier move and the set of legal moves shrinks after each placement. So in our game, which is limited to just the opening placements, each player's payoff is the sum total of the tiles’ probabilities (out of 36) that their settlements lie on. We assume every player follows a simple greedy rule, taking the highest-value open spot every turn.

In Summary

We consider

  • The whole, classic, 19 tile Catan board with 54 possible settlement spots.
  • The standard number tokens, one each of 2 and 12 and two of all others excluding 7.
  • Four players and their two settlements each.
  • That settlements may not occupy adjacent corners.
  • The snake draft (A B C D D C B A) and standard order draft (A B C D A B C D).
  • The ‘true random’ board type and the ‘traditional separated’ board type.

We do not consider

  • Roads attached to settlements.
  • Resources associated with each tile.
  • Trading resource cards between players.
  • Exchanging resources at ports/with the bank.
  • Development cards.
  • The robber and other actions associated with rolling a 7.
  • The overall trajectory that consistent random dice rolls lead the game to have.

Additional Information

  • Four different setups are run, snake draft and standard draft on both 'true random' and 'traditional separated' board types.
  • Each game "i" uses the seed "i", meaning that our results are replicable and the snake and standard drafts are played on exactly the same boards, so the draft order is the only difference between them.
  • Greedy players will break ties between same-value spots at random, but that random number is calculated from the seed so this is repeatable as well.
  • Strategy is limited by the greedy nature of CPU players, they will not strategically block other players as an actual Catan player might.

Project Structure

Everything runs on one small Python game engine (bottom row of Figure 1). It holds all of the rules: the board and which spots are next to each other, the placement rule, the two draft orders and the greedy player. Every move, whether from a computer player or from you on the Play page, goes through the same rule check, so nothing illegal can slip through.

Three parts are built on top of the engine (middle row): the terminal view, the simulations that played the 400,000 games behind the Results page, and a small bridge for this website. The Watch and Play pages run the real Python engine right in your browser using Pyodide, so they follow exactly the same rules as the simulations. 139 automated tests check that it all works, and the README on GitHub explains what every file does and how to run it yourself.
