Results

These results come from 400,000 simulated games. The snake draft and the standard draft were each played on 100,000 'traditional separated' boards and 100,000 'true random' boards. All four players follow a simple greedy rule, taking the highest-value open spot every turn. A player's payoff is the total dice weight of the tiles around their two settlements. Each game uses the same seed in both drafts, so the boards match and the draft order is the only difference.

The fairness gap is how far apart the best and worst seats are, on average. The average spread is how far apart the best and worst scores are inside a single game.

1. Why Snake Nearly Evens Things Out

  • The earlier a seat picks, the better its first spot: A 12.47, B 11.75, C 11.15, and D 10.55.
  • In a snake draft the second round goes back the other way. D places twice in a row and takes the best leftover, and A takes the worst: A 8.24, B 8.80, C 9.38, and D 9.97.
  • Those two effects nearly balance. A's first-pick lead over D is 1.92, and D's second-pick lead over A is 1.73, so A is ahead by only 0.19 points.

2. Snake vs Standard on the Same Boards

  • Standard draft, from best to worst: A 22.44, B 21.14, C 19.94, D 18.80.
  • A snake draft shrinks the best-to-worst gap inside a game by 2.16 points on separated boards and 2.57 on random boards.
  • In a standard draft, A finished first, alone or tied, in all 100,000 games, because each later pick is worth no more than the one before it.
  • Both drafts take the same eight spots in the same order. Only the player who receives each spot changes.

3. Separated vs Random Boards

  • Separated boards cut the snake draft's fairness gap from 0.88 to 0.19, about four times smaller, and the gap inside a game from 1.89 to 1.48.
  • They help a standard draft too, from 4.46 to 3.64, but that draft stays much less balanced.
  • On random boards a 6 and an 8 can touch, which makes one very strong spot. A's first pick is worth 13.39 there, against 12.47 when 6s and 8s are kept apart. The later seats barely change.

Where Players Settle

  • Coast corners touch only one or two tiles, so they are almost never taken.
  • The most-taken spots, about 37% of games each, form a ring around the centre hex rather than sitting on the centre hex's own corners.

Reproduce

Rebuild every number and chart (about 5 minutes; same seeds give the same games):

python main.py experiments --games 100000 --seed 0

Replay any single game from the raw data by its seed, for example:

python main.py play --seed 0 --board separated --draft snake

Limits. These results describe greedy players, a simple heuristic, not equilibrium play. Players who think ahead or block on purpose could change the picture, and then the draft order would also change which spots get taken. The fairness gap is one measure of balance between seats, not proof that a draft is fair in every sense.

Quentin Drucker and Emmett Manning, COM313 Algorithmic Game Theory, Prof. Christine Chung
