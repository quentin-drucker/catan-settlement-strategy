About the project

This project is a study of initial settlement placement in the game Settlers of Catan, where our goal is to determine the 'fairness' of each seat in the placement draft and to what extent different draft types affect this metric.

Background

In Settlers of Catan, the board is a set of hexagonal tiles, each marked with a number from 2 to 12. Every turn two dice are rolled, and every settlement next to a tile with that number collects resources. Numbers near 7 come up most often: a 6 or an 8 can be rolled 5 ways out of 36, a 2 or a 12 only one way. The printed tokens show this with dots under the number.

Settlements sit on the corners where tiles meet, and two settlements may never be on neighbouring corners. So the opening placements matter twice: a good corner pays off all game, and taking it also removes it, and the corners next to it, from everyone who picks later.

That creates a first-mover problem. The player who places first gets the best corner on the board. The official rules already respond to this with a snake order: first settlements go around the table one way, and second settlements come back the other way, so the last player places twice in a row. Fantasy sports drafts use the same idea for the same reason. Whether the snake actually evens things out, and by how much, is an empirical question, and it is the question this project measures.

In game-theory terms, the opening is a finite sequential game with perfect information: players move one at a time, everyone sees every earlier move, and the set of legal moves (open corners) shrinks after each placement. Each player's utility here is the expected production of their two settlements. The game could in principle be solved by backward induction (working out the best move from the last turn back to the first), but the number of possible move sequences is far too large to search completely, so this project does not compute equilibrium play. Instead, every player follows a simple greedy rule, taking the open corner worth the most right now, and the study measures how outcomes differ by seat across many randomly generated boards.

The results are about fairness in practice, not about equilibria. A small gap between seats means that, for these players, no seat is consistently better off. It does not prove the draft is fair for every kind of player, and it is not a Nash equilibrium or price-of-anarchy result.

The model

  • Standard 19-tile board in rows of 3, 4, 5, 4 and 3, with 54 corners (settlement spots).
  • Standard number tokens: one each of 2 and 12, two each of 3 to 6 and 8 to 11, plus one desert tile with no number.
  • Four players, A to D, place two settlements each: eight placements in total.
  • A settlement can't go on a taken corner or on a corner directly next to one. The rule applies to everyone, including a player's own settlements.
  • Snake order: A B C D D C B A. Standard order: A B C D A B C D.
  • Two board types: random (tokens shuffled freely) and separated (reshuffled until no 6 or 8 touches another 6 or 8). Separated is the default.
  • Left out on purpose: roads, trading, development cards, resource types, the robber and dice rolling.

Scoring

A corner's value is the sum of the dice weights of the tiles around it, where the dice weight of a number is how many of the 36 two-dice rolls produce it. A player's payoff is the total value of their two settlements.

How the experiments work

  • Four setups: snake or standard draft, on separated or random boards. 100,000 games each.
  • Game number i uses random seed i in every setup. The board depends only on the seed and the board type, so snake and standard play exactly the same boards, and the draft order is the only difference.
  • Greedy players break ties between equally good corners at random, with a seeded generator, so every game can be replayed exactly.
  • Measures: average payoff per seat with a 95% confidence interval, the fairness gap (best seat average minus worst), the average best-minus-worst spread inside a game, win share (ties split evenly), the value of each seat's first and second pick, and how often each corner is taken.

Limits. Greedy players only look one move ahead and never block on purpose. An optional extension of the project tests a player that also weighs the corners it takes away from opponents.
